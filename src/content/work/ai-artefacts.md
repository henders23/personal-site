## Context

Most of what circulates about AI in education is opinion. What
practitioners actually need is *equipment*: frameworks precise enough to
structure a design decision, and skill files — reusable, inspectable
instruction sets — that make a model behave like a colleague with a
defined job.

The AI Artefacts collection is my working toolbox, published openly on a
Padlet board: the frameworks I design with, and the skill files I hand
to models when I want EAP work done to a standard.

## The problem, precisely

Prompts don't transfer; craft does. A good prompt shared in a webinar
dies on contact with someone else's context, because the thinking that
produced it stayed with the author. Frameworks and skill files are the
transferable form: they encode the *decisions* — what the model may do,
what it must refuse, what good output looks like — so another teacher
can adapt the reasoning, not parrot the incantation.

## Design decisions

**Artefacts, not advice.** Everything on the board is usable as-is:
download the skill file, apply the framework to tomorrow's seminar. If
an item can't survive contact with a real classroom, it doesn't go up.

**Skill files as curriculum.** A skill file that makes a model give
integrity-safe writing feedback is really a statement of what good
feedback *is* — precise enough to execute. Writing them is curriculum
design in a new genre, and reading them teaches the pedagogy as much as
running them does.

**Open by default.** The collection lives on a public board, not behind
a login or a price. The field is moving too fast for hoarding to be
anything but self-importance.

## Where AI fit

Doubly, and honestly: AI is what the artefacts operate, and AI helped
stress-test them — every framework argued against by a model role-playing
a sceptical colleague before any human saw it.

## What I'd change

A Padlet is a wall, not a library — no versioning, no changelog, no way
to see that a framework was superseded. The collection's next home adds
exactly what this page's architecture already believes in: addresses,
cross-links, and an honest record of what changed and why.
