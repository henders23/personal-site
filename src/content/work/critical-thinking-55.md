## Context

"Develop critical thinking" appears in almost every course specification
and almost no lesson plans. The phrase is load-bearing and empty at the
same time: everyone is for it, few can point to the ten minutes of class
time where it happens.

This collection is fifty-five activities that make those ten minutes
concrete — each one a specific, runnable classroom move that exercises a
nameable component of critical thinking: evaluating evidence, spotting
assumptions, weighing competing explanations, reading a claim's hedges.

## The problem, precisely

Critical thinking resources fail in two directions. The theoretical ones
define taxonomies nobody can teach on a Tuesday morning; the practical ones
degrade into puzzle-book filler with no transfer to academic work. The
design constraint was ruthless: every activity had to be **low-prep,
subject-adaptable, and honest about which specific thinking move it
trains** — or it didn't make the fifty-five.

## Design decisions

**One move per activity.** Each activity names the single thinking
operation it exercises. "Critical thinking" is the curriculum; *noticing an
unstated assumption* is the lesson. Teachers can diagnose a group's gap and
reach for the right activity, not the nearest one.

**Authentic academic material as the substrate.** The activities run on the
texts students actually face — abstracts, methods sections, news coverage
of research — because critical thinking about artificial puzzles stays in
the puzzle.

**Designed for adaptation, and it says so.** Every activity ships with its
adjustment points marked: where to swap in discipline content, how to scale
difficulty, what to drop when time is short. A resource that pretends
teachers won't modify it gets modified badly.

<aside class="note">Fifty-five is not a round number on purpose. The
collection stopped when the ideas stopped earning their place, not when it
hit sixty.</aside>

## Where AI fit

AI served as an inexhaustible generator of *material* — flawed arguments to
critique, plausible-but-slippery abstracts, competing explanations of the
same data — under specifications tight enough that each generated text
contained exactly the reasoning flaw the activity needed. Writing those
specifications turned out to be a critical thinking exercise in itself, and
several activities now teach students to do the same: prompt for a flawed
text, then find the flaw.

## What I'd change

The collection is stronger on analysis than on production: plenty of
activities for taking arguments apart, fewer for building better ones.
