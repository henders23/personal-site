## Context

Legitimation Code Theory is some of the most useful theory an EAP tutor
can hold — it explains *why* student writing flatlines into abstraction
or drowns in example, and gives the semantic wave as a designable fix.
It is also notoriously hard to enter: dense apparatus, its own notation
(SG, SD, ER, SR…), and a literature that assumes you're already inside.

Wavelength is a learning app that walks EAP practitioners into LCT
properly: warm, plain-English on the surface, fully cited underneath.

## The problem, precisely

Theory training for teachers usually fails by halving: either the theory
is diluted until it's slogans, or it's delivered intact and lands on no
one. The design problem was to build gradients — every concept reachable
at gist level in a minute, and at citation level for whoever keeps
reading — without ever letting the two layers contradict each other.

## Design decisions

**The navigation is the theory.** LCT theorises knowledge as
constellations, so the app *is* one: five dimensions — Semantics,
Specialization, Autonomy, Density, Temporality — orbit a central hub,
explorable in any order. The structure teaches the first lesson before
any text does: this is not a linear syllabus.

<aside class="note">Every dimension page ends in an annotated Sources
rail, and the Library is ~25 verified, Harvard-referenced sources with a
one-line note on why each earns its place. Theory apps lose teachers'
trust the first time a citation doesn't check out.</aside>

**Every concept has a gradient.** Each dimension opens with the
plain-English gist and "see it in writing" examples, then the cited
idea, the 2×2 code plane, an EAP application panel — and the hardest
material sits behind a "Going deeper" toggle. Depth is opt-in, never
ambushed.

**The Studio: theory you can operate.** The centrepiece is a coding
studio. Study a flatlined student draft against its reworked "waved"
revision — or paste your own paragraph, rate each sentence with semantic
gravity and density sliders, and watch your text's wave redraw live.
The moment a tutor sees their own handout flatline is the moment LCT
stops being literature.

<aside class="note">The app even hands over its own controls: a Tweaks
panel for accent colour, reading font and reduced motion. An app about
how knowledge gets legitimated shouldn't impose its own aesthetics as
law.</aside>

## Where AI fit

AI made the gradients affordable: every concept exists at four depths,
which is a brutal amount of parallel prose. Drafting ran against the
actual LCT literature with every citation verified before inclusion —
the annotated Library exists because "the model said Maton said" is not
a citation. The judgement calls — what counts as the gist of semantic
gravity, which examples are fair to student writers — stayed human.

## What happened

Wavelength is live, tested, and in use as a self-study tool and a
train-the-trainer resource. The Studio is the feature people mention:
several tutors have reported coding their own materials and finding the
flatline the theory predicted.

## What I'd change

The Studio rates sentences by hand, which is the right pedagogy for
learning the codes — but expert tutors outgrow it. The next iteration
adds an AI-suggested first pass the tutor corrects: faster for veterans,
and the corrections themselves become the advanced lesson.
